How to Label Boxes So Unpacking Is Easy

The difference between unpacking in a day and living out of boxes for a month is what's written on the outside of them.

Every box you pack is a box you'll have to find something in later, probably while tired, in a room full of other boxes. Good labelling is the difference between opening the right box first time and opening six wrong ones. It takes thirty seconds per box while you're packing, and it saves hours later. Here's a system that works.

Number every box

Give every box a number, in order, starting at Box 1. Write the number big, on at least two sides. The number is the key to everything else: it connects the physical box to your inventory. Keep a list — on paper or in PackRight — of what number goes with which room and roughly what's in it. When you're looking for the coffee maker on your first morning, you don't want to open boxes at random; you want to look at your list, see "Box 14 — Kitchen — coffee maker, mugs," and go straight to it. PackRight does this automatically: every box has a number, a destination room, and a full contents list you can search.

Room first, contents second

Write the destination room first, in big letters — KITCHEN, BEDROOM, BATHROOM — then a short contents note underneath. The room name is what movers need: it tells them where to put the box without asking you, and it means boxes land in the right room instead of in a pile by the front door. The contents note is what you need: three or four words are enough, like "plates, bowls, mugs" or "towels, shower curtain." Don't try to list everything on the box. That's what the inventory is for. And don't write the room you packed it in if that's different from where it's going — write where it's going.

Write on the side, not just the top

Boxes get stacked. The top of a box disappears under the box above it, and the label with it. Write the room and number on the side of the box, big enough to read from a few steps away. Two sides is better than one. A thick marker works best; pens and pencils are unreadable from any distance. If you're using tape to seal the box, write the label after taping, on the tape or on the box itself, so it doesn't get covered up.

Use colour if it helps, but don't depend on it

Coloured tape or stickers — one colour per room — make boxes easy to spot across a room. Green for kitchen, blue for bedroom, and so on. It works well, especially if movers are helping, because they can sort by colour at a glance. But colour should be extra information, not the only information. Write the room name too, because in dim light, or for anyone who can't tell the colours apart, the words are what matter. And keep the system simple: five or six colours at most, one per room, written down on your inventory so everyone uses the same ones.

Mark fragile, heavy, and "open first"

Three marks are worth adding. "FRAGILE" on any box with breakables, in big letters on two sides — see our fragile packing guide for how to pack those boxes in the first place. "HEAVY" on boxes that are awkward to lift, so nobody grabs one by surprise and hurts their back. And "OPEN FIRST" on the handful of boxes you need on day one — the First Night Essentials box, the kitchen basics, the bedding. Those boxes go in the truck last and come out first, and they get opened before anything else. Everything else can wait.

Print labels for the boxes that matter

For boxes with a lot in them, a printed label beats handwriting: a big box number, the room, and the full contents list, all legible. PackRight Pro can print a label for any box straight from the app — number, destination room, contents, and a fragile warning if anything inside is fragile. Stick it on the side of the box and you can read exactly what's in it without opening it. Even without printed labels, the habit is the same: number, room, contents, and the three marks. Do it for every box, every time, and unpacking stops being a search and starts being a checklist.
