How to Pack Fragile Items for Moving

Most breakage doesn't happen because something was dropped. It happens because things shifted inside the box. Pack so nothing can move.

Fragile packing has one rule that matters more than any other: nothing inside the box should be able to move. If you shake a sealed box gently and hear anything shift, it isn't packed yet. Everything else — the wrapping, the box size, the padding — is just a way of making that true.

Start with the right box

Use a sturdy, undamaged box, and use a small one. Big boxes full of fragile things are heavy, hard to carry, and more likely to be set down hard. A small box with a few well-wrapped items in it survives a move far better than a big box stuffed full. Tape the bottom of the box with two or three strips, not one, and run a strip across the middle for good measure. Line the bottom with a thick layer of crumpled packing paper or bubble wrap — at least a few inches. That layer is the shock absorber for everything above it.

Wrap every piece individually

This is the step people skip, and it's the step that matters most. Two unwrapped glasses touching each other will chip; two wrapped ones won't. Wrap each piece in packing paper, bubble wrap, or a clean towel, and tape the wrapping closed so it doesn't come undone in the box. For glasses and stemware, stuff paper inside the glass first, then wrap the outside. For plates, wrap each one and stand them on edge, like records, instead of stacking them flat. For picture frames and mirrors, wrap them flat, tape an X of masking tape across the glass face to hold it together if it does crack, and pack them in a flat box or a purpose-made mirror box with padding on both sides. For lamps, remove the shade and the bulb, wrap them separately, and pack the base upright.

Pack in layers, fill every gap

Place the heaviest wrapped items at the bottom and lighter ones on top. Put a layer of crumpled paper between layers. Fill every gap — every single one — with crumpled paper, towels, or soft clothing. Gaps are where movement happens, and movement is where breakage happens. When the box is full, put a final thick layer of padding on top before you seal it. The box should feel firm, not squishy, when you press the lid. If it gives, add more padding.

Label it like it matters, because it does

Write "FRAGILE" in big letters on at least two sides of the box, plus the room it belongs in and a short note about what's inside — "Glasses — Kitchen" beats "Fragile" on its own. Add "This side up" with an arrow if the contents need to stay upright. In PackRight, mark the items as fragile in the app too, so the box shows a fragile badge and anyone unpacking knows to open it carefully. Our box labelling guide covers a full system for this.

What not to do

Don't use newspaper as your only wrapping for anything you care about — the ink transfers, and it tears more easily than packing paper. Don't pack fragile items with heavy, hard things in the same box. Don't leave a fragile box for the bottom of a stack, and don't let anyone stack a heavy box on top of one marked fragile. Don't pack anything truly irreplaceable — family photos, one-of-a-kind keepsakes, important documents — in the moving truck at all. Carry those with you in the car. And don't rush this job at midnight the night before the move. Fragile packing is slow work; give it an afternoon, not an hour.

The shake test

Before you tape any fragile box shut, close the flaps and shake it gently side to side. If you hear or feel anything move, open it and add padding until it doesn't. Then tape it, label it, and set it somewhere it won't get stacked on. Do that for every fragile box, and your breakage rate drops to almost nothing.
