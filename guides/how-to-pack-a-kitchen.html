How to Pack a Kitchen for Moving

The kitchen is the hardest room to pack and the one where most things break. Do it last, do it in the right order, and your dishes will arrive in one piece.

The kitchen has more fragile, awkward, heavy, and oddly-shaped things in it than any other room, and you use it every day right up to the move. That's why it goes last. Pack it in three passes: the stuff you rarely use, the everyday stuff, and a small survival kit you keep out until moving day.

First pass: declutter and pack what you don't use

Start by getting rid of things. Kitchens collect duplicates — three spatulas, two cheese graters, a drawer of takeout cutlery, gadgets used once. If you haven't used it in a year, you won't miss it. Expired food, open bags, and half-used spices go in the bin. Donate unopened non-perishables to a food bank. Then pack the rarely-used things: baking dishes, serving platters, cookbooks, seasonal items, and the small appliances that live at the back of a cupboard. Wrap each one, label the box with its contents and "Kitchen," and move it to your staging area. This pass can happen two or three weeks before the move.

How to wrap dishes and glasses

Use small or medium boxes for dishes — a large box full of plates is too heavy to lift safely. Line the bottom of the box with a thick layer of crumpled packing paper. Wrap each plate individually in packing paper and stand plates on their edges, like records in a crate, never stacked flat in a tall pile. Plates stacked flat put all the weight on the bottom one; plates on edge spread it. Fill every gap with crumpled paper so nothing shifts. Bowls nest with paper between each one. Glasses get wrapped individually too, with paper stuffed inside the glass, and packed upright in a box with dividers if you have them. Put a layer of paper on top, seal the box, and mark it "FRAGILE — Kitchen — This side up." Don't overfill: if you can't lift the box comfortably, take something out. For the full technique, see our guide to packing fragile items.

Pots, pans, and small appliances

Stack pots and pans with a sheet of packing paper between each one so they don't scratch each other, and pack lids separately, wrapped, standing on edge. Small appliances go back in their original boxes if you kept them — that's what the moulded packaging is for. If not, wrap them in a towel or packing paper, pack them upright, and fill the gaps so they can't slide around. Remove loose parts like blender blades and food processor bowls and wrap them separately. Tape the cord to the appliance so it doesn't snag on other boxes.

Knives, sharp things, and the pantry

Knives are a safety issue, not just a breakage issue. Wrap each blade in several layers of packing paper or a folded towel, tape it flat so it can't slide out, and pack them together in a clearly marked box, blades all facing the same way. Do the same for scissors, peelers, and graters. For the pantry, pack unopened jars and cans in small boxes, heavy things on the bottom, and leave open packets behind — they spill, and they're cheap to replace. Don't pack cleaning products with food, and don't pack anything flammable or pressurised; movers won't take those, and you shouldn't put them in a truck yourself either. Check lids on anything liquid, put bottles in plastic bags in case they leak, and keep them upright.

The last-day kitchen kit

Keep out one box that stays with you until moving day: a couple of plates, mugs, and cutlery per person, a kettle or coffee maker, dish soap, a sponge, paper towels, a bin bag, and a basic tool like a screwdriver. Pack everything else, and put this kit in your First Night Essentials box on the last night so your first morning in the new place starts with coffee, not a hunt through twenty boxes. When you unpack, do the kitchen first — everything else in the house is easier once you can make a meal and wash a dish.
