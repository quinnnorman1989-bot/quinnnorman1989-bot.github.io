Firewood Guide

Seasoned softwood, drying and stacking, and keeping a camp warm through winter — in plain language.

At an off-grid camp, firewood is not a hobby. It is the heat, and in a cold snap it is the difference between a comfortable night and a miserable one. This guide covers the softwood most northern camps actually burn — spruce, balsam fir, jack pine, and poplar — how to dry it, how to stack it, and what else you need to think about before winter arrives.

What softwood is worth

Heat is measured in BTU, and a full cord is the standard measure of firewood. A full cord is a stack 4 feet high, 4 feet deep, and 8 feet long. Seasoned softwood gives roughly these amounts of heat per full cord: spruce about 15.5 million BTU, balsam fir about 15 million BTU, jack pine about 17 million BTU, and poplar about 13.5 million BTU. Jack pine is the hottest of the four, and poplar is the coolest. That does not make poplar useless — it lights easily and burns well when it is dry — but you will burn through it faster, so you need more of it for the same amount of heat.

Seasoning and drying

Seasoning means letting cut and split wood dry in the open air until most of the water is gone. Softwood seasons faster than hardwood, but it still takes time. Cut and split your wood well before you need it, ideally in the spring or early summer for the following winter. Split wood dries far faster than whole rounds, because splitting opens up the inside of the log to the air. Cut logs to the length your stove takes, usually around 16 inches, and split anything thicker than your wrist.

Stack the wood off the ground on pallets, rails, or old poles so air can move underneath it. Keep the stack in a sunny, windy spot if you can, and cover only the top — a tarp over the top keeps rain and snow off, but covering the sides traps damp air and slows drying. Leave the sides open. Stack bark side up on the top row if you want a little extra rain protection. Wood that is ready to burn feels light for its size, the ends show cracks, and two pieces knocked together make a sharp, hollow sound instead of a dull thud.

Stacking and storing

A good stack is straight, stable, and easy to reach in deep snow. Build it in rows with small gaps between pieces so air can move through. Do not stack wood tight against the cabin wall — it holds moisture against the building and gives insects a bridge inside. Keep a smaller working pile close to the door, under cover, so you are not digging through a snowdrift for every armload on a cold night.

Use the firewood calculator on the main toolkit page to work out how many cords a stack holds and how many days of heat it gives you at your daily heat use. It is better to have a cord left over in spring than to run short in February, when cutting and drying more wood is not an option.

Burning it well

Burn seasoned wood only, and give the fire enough air. A slow, smouldering fire in a damped-down stove feels thrifty, but it makes more smoke and more creosote, and it gives you less heat from each log. A hot, clean burn with a good bed of coals is more efficient and kinder to the chimney. Do not burn painted or treated wood, plywood, or household rubbish in a camp stove — treated wood can give off harmful fumes, and rubbish leaves residues you do not want in the flue or in the air you breathe.

Chimney cleaning

Creosote is the sticky, tar-like deposit that builds up inside a chimney when wood smoke cools on its way up the flue. It builds up fastest when wood is damp, when fires are kept low and smouldering, and when the chimney is cold. Enough creosote can catch fire inside the chimney, and a chimney fire is a serious emergency at a camp with no fire department nearby.

Clean the chimney before the heating season starts, and check it again during the winter if you are burning every day. A chimney brush sized for your flue, used from the top or the bottom depending on your setup, will remove loose soot and creosote. Burning only seasoned wood and keeping fires hot and clean is the best way to slow creosote down in the first place.

Backup heat

Wood heat fails in ways that are easy to overlook until they happen. You run out of wood, the stove needs a part, the chimney is blocked, or you are away and the fire goes out and the camp freezes. Every off-grid camp needs a backup heat source that does not depend on the same fuel. That might be a propane heater, an electric heater for when the generator is running, or simply a second stove. Whatever it is, test it before you need it, keep its fuel separate and full, and know how to start it in the dark in cold weather.
