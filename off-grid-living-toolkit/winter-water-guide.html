Winter Water Guide

Water totes, freezing, draining lines, and hot water at an off-grid camp in winter.

Water is the hardest thing to keep working at a winter camp. Heat can be fixed with more wood, and power can be fixed with more fuel, but water freezes, and once a line or a tote is frozen solid it stays that way until the weather changes. The trick is to plan for freezing before it happens, to know exactly how much water you have, and to keep the system simple enough that you can fix it yourself.

Water totes

Many off-grid camps store water in large plastic totes kept outside. A big tote holds a lot of water, which is good, but a big tote also freezes from the outside in, and once the top and sides are frozen, the water in the middle is hard to reach. Totes left full outside in a hard winter can freeze solid, and a tote that is full when it freezes can split or bulge as the ice expands. That is why some camps keep their main tote only partly full in winter, or keep a smaller amount of water inside the heated cabin where it cannot freeze.

How many days your water lasts

The first question to answer is how much water you actually use. A frugal camp figure is about 10 gallons per person per day for drinking, cooking, and basic washing. Flushing, laundry, and showers push that figure much higher, so 10 gallons is a planning floor, not a comfortable average. Two people at 10 gallons each use 20 gallons a day, so a 250-gallon tote lasts about 12 days at that rate, and less if showers or laundry are included.

Freezing and draining lines

Any water line that runs through an unheated space will freeze. That includes lines under the floor, along an outside wall, and in a crawl space. The way to deal with that is to drain what can freeze, insulate the lines that cannot be drained, and keep the lines you use every day inside the heated part of the building wherever possible. Before a cold snap, drain garden hoses, outside taps, and any line you will not use until spring.

Hot water and the propane tankless heater

Hot water at camp usually comes from a propane tankless water heater, and the one used at this camp is the Bosch GWH 260 PN L. A tankless heater only heats water when it is flowing, so it needs a pressurized water feed to fire at all. This model needs a minimum flow of 0.5 gallons per minute of pressurized water before it will turn on. That is an important detail, because it rules out some simple water setups.

A pitcher pump alone will not run it. A pitcher pump is a hand pump that lifts water a little at a time, with no pressure behind it, so the heater never sees the flow it needs and never fires. To run a tankless heater you need a pressurized feed — for example a holding tank with a pump, an elevated gravity tank high enough to give pressure, or a well pump with a pressure tank. Each of those has its own trade-offs in cost, complexity, and freezing risk, and the choice is still open at this camp. Do not buy water-side fittings for the heater until the feed is decided, because the fittings depend on how the water gets to the heater.

Use the propane usage calculator to work out how much propane a heater or other appliance uses per day, and how long a tank lasts. Propane use is easy to underestimate in winter, when hot water and heat both run longer, so check the numbers before a cold stretch rather than during it.

Gas work and safety

In Ontario, gas work must be done by a TSSA-licensed gas fitter. That covers installing a propane tankless heater, changing gas lines, and connecting gas appliances. It is not a job to do yourself to save money, and it is not a job to learn by trial and error. A bad gas connection can leak, and propane is heavier than air, so a leak can pool in low spots and become dangerous. A licensed fitter will also check the venting, the gas pressure, and the clearances, all of which matter for a heater that burns gas inside or beside a cabin.

A simple winter water routine

The camps that get through winter with the least trouble are the ones with a routine. Check the tote level every day, and write down how many days are left. Keep a jug of drinking water inside, so a frozen outside valve never leaves you with nothing to drink. Drain hoses after every use. Keep a spare valve, a few fittings, and a bucket on hand. And before you leave the camp for more than a day, drain what can freeze and leave the heat on low or the lines drained — whichever your setup needs.
