Generator & Solar Power Guide

Generator-only power, fuel, batteries in the cold, solar, and keeping the lights and internet on at camp.

Many off-grid camps run on generator power only. There is no grid connection, no power line to fall back on, and when the generator stops, everything stops — lights, phones, the water pump, and the internet. Power at camp is therefore a routine, not a one-time setup. You need to know how much fuel you use, how long a tank lasts, how to keep the generator running, and how to look after batteries in cold weather.

Generator-only power

A generator turns fuel into electricity, and it does it best when it is running at a sensible load. Running a big generator for a long time just to charge a phone wastes fuel. Running it too hard, with every appliance on at once, can overload it. The practical approach is to run the generator in blocks: a run in the morning and a run in the evening, charging batteries, running the water pump, and doing the heavy jobs while it is on, and letting the batteries carry the light loads in between.

Use the generator runtime calculator on the main toolkit page to check it: put in the tank size, the fuel use per hour at your usual load, and the hours you run per day, and it tells you the runtime per tank and the days per tank. If the real number is very different from the calculator, trust the real number — your load, your fuel, and your generator are the ones that count.

Fuel use and servicing

Fuel use depends on load, and load changes through the day. A generator sipping fuel at a light load can use much more when a heater, a pump, or a power tool kicks in. That is why fuel use per hour is best measured at your usual load, not at idle and not at full power.

Batteries in cold weather

Batteries hate the cold. A battery that starts a generator easily in summer can be weak in winter, and a battery left flat in the cold can be damaged. Keep batteries charged, and keep them as warm as you reasonably can — inside the cabin is better than outside in a cold shed. A dead battery in minus 20 degrees is a different problem than a dead battery in summer, because everything else is harder too, and your hands are cold.

Different battery types behave differently in the cold, and they have different usable depths of discharge. Lithium batteries can generally be used down to about 80 percent of their capacity, while lead-acid batteries should only be used down to about 50 percent if you want them to last. The solar and battery estimator lets you put in your daily electricity use, the sun hours, the days of backup you want, the system voltage, and the usable depth, and it estimates the solar panel size and battery bank you need. Size for your worst month, not your best — winter sun hours are much lower, and a system that works in July can fall short in January.

Solar at camp

Solar panels can take some of the load off the generator, especially in the longer days of spring, summer, and autumn. They are quiet, they have no fuel to buy, and they work while you are away. But they are not a magic fix for winter power. Short days, low sun, snow on the panels, and long nights all cut output sharply. Keep panels clear of snow, angle them for the low winter sun if you can, and remember that solar is a supplement to the generator in winter, not a replacement for it, unless the system is sized for winter from the start.

Starlink Mini power

Internet at camp now often comes from a Starlink Mini, and its power needs are worth knowing in advance. The Mini runs on 12 to 48 volt DC power, and it uses about 25 to 40 watts on average, with a maximum of about 60 watts. That is a modest load, but it is a constant one while the dish is on, so it adds up over a day. At 40 watts, that is about 960 watt-hours in a day, which is a real share of a small off-grid system.

A simple power routine

The camps that keep the power on are the ones with a routine. Run the generator at set times, charge the batteries fully, check the fuel level every day, and keep a spare battery, a spare spark plug, and a can of fuel in reserve. Know which loads are essential and which can wait — lights, heat controls, and the water pump matter more than a second screen. And keep a flashlight and a charged phone power bank in the cabin, because the night the generator will not start is the night you will need them most.
