Camp Shower Guide

Hot water at an off-grid camp — a propane tankless heater, a pressurised water feed, and doing the gas work properly.

A hot shower at camp changes winter living. It is also one of the easiest camp projects to get wrong, because a tankless propane heater will not fire at all unless its water supply is right. This guide is about a camp shower built around a Bosch GWH 260 PN L propane tankless water heater, and the water feed it needs to work.

The heater, and the one number that matters

The Bosch GWH 260 PN L is a propane tankless water heater: it heats water only when you turn the tap on, and it has no tank to keep hot. To fire, it needs a minimum flow of 0.5 gallons per minute, and that water has to be pressurised. That is the whole project in one sentence. If the water arrives as a trickle, or at no pressure, the heater will not light, and you will get a cold shower no matter how much propane is in the tank. Use the water supply calculator and propane usage calculator on the main toolkit page to work out how long your water and propane last.

Why a pitcher pump will not run it

A pitcher pump — a hand pump that lifts water a jug at a time — will not run this heater. It cannot deliver a steady 0.5 gallons per minute, and it cannot pressurise the line. The heater needs a real feed: water under pressure, flowing steadily, every time you open the tap. Decide the water feed first, before you buy shower-side fittings.

Feed option 1: holding tank plus a 12V pump

One common camp setup is a holding tank — a water tote or tank kept at camp — with a 12V pump that pushes water from the tank to the heater. The pump gives you the pressure and the steady flow the heater needs, and it runs on the same 12V power as much other camp gear. You need a charged battery or the generator running when you shower, and you need to keep the tank filled.

Feed option 2: an elevated gravity tank

A second option is a tank raised above the shower, so gravity gives you the pressure. No pump, no power, no moving parts. The catch is height: you need enough height to get real pressure at the shower head, and you need a way to fill a tank that high. A gravity feed can work well, but it is not free — the tank, the stand, and the filling all have to be planned, and the flow still has to reach 0.5 gallons per minute at the heater. Test the flow at the shower head before you call the job done.

Feed option 3: well pump plus a pressure tank

A third option is a well pump with a pressure tank, the same kind of setup a house on a well uses. The pump fills the pressure tank, and the tank delivers steady, pressurised water to the shower. This is the most work to install, and it suits a camp that is staying put and already has a well.

Which feed to choose

No feed is decided here, because the right one depends on your camp: how your water arrives, what power you have, and how permanent the shower is. A tote-and-pump setup suits a camp that hauls water in. A gravity tank suits a camp with a good high spot and a simple setup. A well pump suits a camp with a well and steady power. What does not change is the requirement: a steady, pressurised flow of at least 0.5 gallons per minute. Pick the feed that gives you that, and do not buy shower-side fittings until you have.

Winter draining

A camp shower that freezes will break. Water left in the heater, the pipes, or the shower head expands when it freezes, and it cracks fittings and the heater itself. Before the cold sets in, drain the heater, the lines, and the head, and know where every low point is. If you use it in winter, keep the shower room warm enough that nothing freezes, and drain it whenever you leave camp for more than a day or two.

Gas work: the rule in Ontario

In Ontario, gas installation must be done by a TSSA-licensed gas fitter. That is not a suggestion, and it is not a job to learn by trying. The propane supply, the connections to the heater, and the venting all have to be done by someone licensed to do them. A licensed fitter will also check that the heater is vented properly and that the propane supply is safe. Do not touch the gas fittings yourself, and do not let anyone unlicensed do it either. The water side is yours to plan; the gas side belongs to a licensed fitter.
