Winterizing Checklist

The full version — water, heat, power, propane, access, and supplies, done before the cold arrives, not during it.

Winter at an off-grid camp does not forgive a job left half done. A pipe that freezes, a battery that dies, or a road that closes turns a small problem into a cold night with no easy fix. This is the full winterizing checklist, expanding the short version on the main toolkit page. Do it before the cold arrives. Use the calculators on the main page — firewood, solar and battery, generator, propane, and water — to put real numbers behind each section.

Water: drain, insulate, and know your days

Water is the first thing winter breaks. Drain anything that can freeze and will not be used: outdoor lines, hoses, and any pipe in an unheated space. Insulate the exposed lines you keep, and know where every low point is, because that is where water sits and freezes. Know how many days your totes actually last — use the water supply calculator with your real daily use, not a guess, and keep a margin for a storm that stops a water run. If you have a camp shower, drain the heater, the lines, and the head before the cold, as covered in the camp shower guide. A frozen shower heater is an expensive lesson. Keep drinking water where it will not freeze.

Heat: seasoned wood, a clean chimney, and a backup

Heat is the stove, the wood, and the chimney. Burn seasoned wood only — wet wood gives less heat, makes more smoke, and coats the chimney with creosote. Clean the chimney before the heating season, and check it again mid-winter if you burn every day. Stock the wood early, stacked off the ground with the top covered and the sides open. See the firewood guide for the softwood you will burn and how much heat a cord gives. Have a backup heat source that does not depend on the same fuel, and test it before you need it, in the dark, in cold weather.

Power: generator serviced, fuel stabilised, batteries charged

When the generator is your only power, it is not a machine you can ignore until it fails. Service it before winter: oil, air filter, spark plug, and a check that it starts easily when cold. Stabilise the fuel, and keep enough of it — a dead generator in minus 20 is a different problem than in summer. Keep batteries charged, because a dead battery in the cold is often a dead battery for good, and a battery that is flat will not take a charge when you need it most. Plan your loads: the Starlink Mini uses about 25 to 40 watts on average, so decide when it needs to be on. See the generator and solar guide and the Starlink guide, and use the generator runtime calculator and solar estimator to plan fuel and battery capacity.

Propane: licensed fitter only, and check the tanks

Propane heats, cooks, and runs the tankless shower, so check it before winter. Make sure tanks are filled and the connections are sound, and know how long a tank lasts at the rate you use it — the propane usage calculator will tell you. Any gas install or change must be done by a licensed gas fitter, and in Ontario that means a TSSA-licensed gas fitter. Do not touch gas fittings yourself, and do not let anyone unlicensed do it. If a fitting, a hose, or a regulator looks damaged or smells of gas, stop and get a licensed fitter to look at it. Propane is not a job to learn by trying.

Access and vehicle: road, path, and a way to get help

Winter makes small problems big, and the road is one of them. Make sure you can get in and out in snow: the road, the path to the door, and a place to park that will not trap the vehicle. Keep the vehicle in a state to start in the cold — a good battery, fuel, and a way to boost it if it will not turn over. Keep a shovel, sand or grit, and a tow strap where you can reach them. Have a way to get help if a vehicle will not start: a charged phone, the Starlink dish running, and someone who knows where you are.

Food and supplies: stock for a storm, not for a day

Stock food and supplies for a storm, not for a day. Keep enough dry food, water, and fuel for several days without a trip to town, because a storm can close the road for longer than you expect. Keep a first aid kit, any medication you need, warm clothing, and a way to cook and heat water if the power is out. Check the supplies before winter: batteries, candles or lamps, matches, and a radio or a charged phone. A camp stocked for a week can wait out a storm; a camp stocked for a day has to drive in bad weather.
